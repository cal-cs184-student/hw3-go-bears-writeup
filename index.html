Overview

In this homework we built a path tracer that renders physically based images of 3D scenes. First, we generate rays from the camera through each pixel and find the closest triangle or sphere that each ray hits. Then we build a bounding volume hierarchy (BVH), so that each ray is tested against a few primitives instead of all of them, which makes large meshes possible to render. Next, we compute direct lighting with Monte Carlo integration, first by sampling the hemisphere uniformly and then by sampling the lights. After that, we add global illumination: paths bounce several times in the scene, and Russian roulette stops them without bias. Finally, adaptive sampling stops sampling a pixel when its value has converged. The main thing we learned is that a realistic image comes from one simple equation, estimated with random samples: most of the work is choosing these samples well, so that the noise goes down quickly, and being careful with coordinate systems and probabilities.

Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

Ray generation and primitive intersection

For every pixel we want to estimate how much light reaches the camera through it. In Part 1 this comes down to two steps: build rays that leave the camera through the pixel, then find the first surface each ray hits.

Sampling a pixel. In PathTracer::raytrace_pixel(x, y), \((x, y)\) is the bottom-left corner of the pixel in image space. We draw ns_aa random offsets in \([0,1]^2\) with gridSampler->get_sample(), add each one to the corner, and divide by sampleBuffer.w and sampleBuffer.h to get normalized coordinates in \([0,1]^2\). Each point is turned into a ray by the camera and traced with est_radiance_global_illumination. We average the returned radiances and store the result with sampleBuffer.update_pixel. Using several random positions inside the pixel instead of only its center is what gives us antialiasing.

Camera rays. Camera::generate_ray(x, y) works in camera space, where the camera sits at the origin and looks down \(-Z\). The sensor is the plane \(z = -1\), going from \((-\tan\frac{hFov}{2}, -\tan\frac{vFov}{2}, -1)\) at the bottom left to \((\tan\frac{hFov}{2}, \tan\frac{vFov}{2}, -1)\) at the top right. The normalized point is mapped linearly onto that rectangle: \[ d_{cam} = \left( (2x-1)\tan\frac{hFov}{2},\ (2y-1)\tan\frac{vFov}{2},\ -1 \right) \] hFov and vFov are given in degrees, so we convert them to radians first. We then rotate \(d_{cam}\) to world space with c2w and normalize it. The origin of the ray is pos, the camera position, which is already in world coordinates. Finally we set min_t = nClip and max_t = fClip.

Intersecting primitives. A ray is \(r(t) = o + t\,d\) with \(t\) between min_t and max_t. Every primitive has has_intersection, which only says whether the ray hits it, and intersect, which also fills an Intersection with \(t\), the normal, the primitive and its BSDF. When a hit is accepted we set max_t to its \(t\). Any primitive tested later then has to be closer to count, so at the end the Intersection holds the closest hit no matter in which order the primitives were tested. For now the hit is colored with its normal, using \(n \cdot 0.5 + 0.5\) as RGB.

Spheres. Plugging \(r(t)\) into \(\|p - c\|^2 = R^2\) gives a quadratic \(at^2 + bt + c' = 0\) with \(a = d \cdot d\), \(b = 2(o-c)\cdot d\) and \(c' = \|o-c\|^2 - R^2\). Sphere::test returns false if the discriminant is negative, otherwise it returns both roots \(t_1 \le t_2\). We keep \(t_1\) if it is in [min_t, max_t], otherwise \(t_2\) if it is (this happens when the ray starts inside the sphere), otherwise there is no hit. The normal is \((p - c)/\|p - c\|\).

Triangle intersection algorithm

We implemented the Möller–Trumbore algorithm from lecture. A point of the triangle can be written with barycentric coordinates as \((1-b_1-b_2)p_1 + b_1p_2 + b_2p_3\). Setting it equal to \(o + t\,d\) gives three equations (x, y, z) with three unknowns \(t, b_1, b_2\). With \(E_1 = p_2 - p_1\), \(E_2 = p_3 - p_1\), \(S = o - p_1\), \(S_1 = d \times E_2\) and \(S_2 = S \times E_1\), Cramer's rule gives \[ \begin{pmatrix} t \ b_1 \ b_2 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{S_1 \cdot E_1} \begin{pmatrix} S_2 \cdot E_2 \ S_1 \cdot S \ S_2 \cdot d \end{pmatrix} \] We compute \(S_1 \cdot E_1\) once and divide by it three times. If it is close to zero (we use \(|S_1 \cdot E_1| \le 10^{-8}\)), the ray is parallel to the triangle and we return false. Otherwise the hit is valid when \(t\) is in [min_t, max_t] and \(b_1 \ge 0\), \(b_2 \ge 0\), \(1 - b_1 - b_2 \ge 0\), meaning the point is inside the triangle and not just in its plane. In that case we set max_t = t, and intersect also fills the Intersection: \(t\), this, get_bsdf(), and the normal interpolated from the vertex normals with the same weights, \(n = (1-b_1-b_2)n_1 + b_1n_2 + b_2n_3\).

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

Walk through our BVH construction algorithm

Without acceleration, every ray is tested against every primitive, so the cost of a ray grows linearly with the number of triangles. A bounding volume hierarchy (BVH) is a binary tree of axis-aligned boxes. Each node stores the bounding box of all the primitives under it, and only the leaves store primitives. If a ray misses the box of a node, it cannot hit anything inside it, so we skip the whole subtree with a single box test.

BVHAccel::construct_bvh(start, end, max_leaf_size) builds the tree recursively on the range [start, end) of the primitive vector:

  1. We loop over the primitives of the range and expand a BBox with the bounding box of each one. We create a new BVHNode with this box.
  2. If the range has at most max_leaf_size primitives (4 by default), the node is a leaf. We store start and end in it and return it.
  3. Otherwise we choose the split axis: the axis (x, y or z) along which the box has the largest extent.
  4. We choose the split point (see the heuristic below) and reorder the range with std::partition. The primitives whose bounding box centroid is below the split point go to the front of the range, and the other ones go to the back. std::partition returns an iterator to the first primitive of the second group.
  5. We call construct_bvh on [start, split) for the left child and on [split, end) for the right child.

Because we reorder the primitives in place, each leaf is just a sub-range of the same vector, and we never copy primitives into new vectors.

Splitting heuristic. We split at the middle of the node's bounding box along its longest axis (the spatial median), and we decide on which side a primitive goes with the centroid of its own bounding box. Splitting along the longest axis gives children that are closer to cubes than thin slabs, so their boxes are small and a ray is less likely to hit both. The midpoint is cheap to compute (it is bbox.centroid(), so we do not need to sort), and the build takes only a few milliseconds even for CBlucy.dae (0.09 s for 133,796 triangles). This heuristic has one failure case: if all the centroids are on the same side of the midpoint (for example when many primitives have almost the same centroid), one child is empty and the recursion would never end. In that case we split at the middle of the range instead (start + n / 2). Both children then have about half of the primitives, so the range always gets smaller and the recursion stops.

Part 3: Direct Illumination

Walk through both implementations of the direct lighting function

Direct lighting is the light that goes from a light source to a point \(p\) of a surface, and then from \(p\) to the camera. It is given by the reflection equation: \[ L_{out}(p, \omega_o) = \int_{H^2} f(\omega_i \to \omega_o)\, L_i(p, \omega_i) \cos\theta_i \, d\omega_i \] We estimate this integral with Monte Carlo integration: we pick \(N\) random directions \(\omega_j\) with a probability density \(pdf(\omega_j)\) and compute \[ \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \frac{f(\omega_j \to \omega_o)\, L_i(p, \omega_j) \cos\theta_j}{pdf(\omega_j)} \] The two implementations use this same formula. The only difference is how the directions \(\omega_j\) are chosen.

Common parts. The BSDF works in the local frame of the hit point, where the normal is the \(z\) axis. o2w goes from local to world coordinates and w2o from world to local, so \(\cos\theta = \omega_i.z\) in local coordinates. Our materials are diffuse, so DiffuseBSDF::f returns the same value for every direction: \(\rho / \pi\), where \(\rho\) is the albedo (reflectance). We divide by \(\pi\) because \(\int_{H^2} \cos\theta \, d\omega = \pi\), so a surface never reflects more light than it receives. zero_bounce_radiance returns get_emission() of the hit surface, which is not zero only for lights. Every new ray that starts from a hit point uses min_t = EPS_F, so it does not hit the same surface again because of rounding errors. one_bounce_radiance calls one of the two functions below, depending on the -H flag.

Uniform hemisphere sampling (estimate_direct_lighting_hemisphere). For each sample:

  1. we get a direction \(\omega_i\) in local coordinates with hemisphereSampler->get_sample();
  2. we convert it to world coordinates with o2w and cast a ray from the hit point in this direction;
  3. if the ray hits a surface, \(L_i\) is the emission of that surface. It is zero unless the surface is a light, so only rays that reach a light add something;
  4. we add \(f \cdot L_i \cdot \cos\theta / pdf\) with \(pdf = \frac{1}{2\pi}\), because all directions have the same probability and the unit hemisphere has an area of \(2\pi\).

At the end we divide the sum by the number of samples.

Light sampling (estimate_direct_lighting_importance). Only directions that point to a light can give direct light, so we sample points on the lights instead of random directions. For each light of the scene:

  1. we take 1 sample if it is a point light (is_delta_light()), because all samples would be the same, and ns_area_light samples for an area light;
  2. for each sample, sample_L(hit_p, &wi, &distToLight, &pdf) returns the radiance of the light and gives us the direction \(\omega_i\) to a random point on the light (in world coordinates), the distance to this point and the pdf;
  3. we convert \(\omega_i\) to local coordinates with w2o. If \(\cos\theta \le 0\), the light is behind the surface and we skip the sample;
  4. we cast a shadow ray from the hit point to the light, with min_t = EPS_F and max_t = distToLight - EPS_F, so the ray stops just before the light. If bvh->has_intersection returns true, an object blocks the light and the sample gives 0;
  5. otherwise we add \(f \cdot L_i \cdot \cos\theta / pdf\).

We divide the sum of each light by its number of samples, then add the results of all the lights.

Soft shadows with 1, 4, 16 and 64 light rays

CBbunny.dae with light sampling and 1 sample per pixel. A point in the soft shadow under the bunny sees only a part of the area light. With 1 light ray, we test the visibility of only one point of the light, so the answer is "fully lit" or "fully dark". The shadow is then a mix of black and white pixels, without a clear shape. With more light rays, each pixel averages more visibility tests and gets closer to the real fraction of the light that it sees. The standard deviation of a Monte Carlo estimate decreases as \(1/\sqrt{N}\), so 64 rays give 8 times less noise than 1 ray. Areas that are fully lit or fully in shadow change much less, because every sample gives the same visibility there.

Uniform hemisphere sampling vs. light sampling

Both methods estimate the same integral, so they converge to the same image, but with the same number of samples hemisphere sampling is much noisier. The light covers a small part of the hemisphere, so most random directions miss it and give 0, and the few that hit it give a large value. The average is right, but it changes a lot from one pixel to the next. With light sampling every sample points to a light, so the only noise left comes from the shadow test, and it is visible only in the soft shadows. Hemisphere sampling also cannot render point lights: a random direction has zero probability to hit a single point, so dragon.dae is completely black with -H. One more difference is the ceiling near the light. It gets a little light with hemisphere sampling and stays black with light sampling. The area light only emits downwards: sample_L returns 0 for a point behind the light, but in hemisphere sampling a ray from the ceiling that hits the back of the light still reads its emission.

Problems we ran into

  • Our first DiffuseBSDF::f depended on \(\omega_i\). A diffuse surface reflects light the same way in all directions, so \(f\) must be a constant, \(\rho / \pi\).
  • In light sampling, sample_L gives \(\omega_i\) in world coordinates. At first we used it directly in the BSDF and then used the local version for the shadow ray, which is wrong in both places. We fixed it by keeping two variables, one for each coordinate system.

Part 4: Global Illumination

Walk through your implementation of the indirect lighting function

In Part 3, the incoming light \(L_i\) was only the emission of the surface hit by the new ray. But this surface also reflects the light it receives from the rest of the scene. So \(L_i(p, \omega_i)\) is the outgoing light \(L_{out}(p', -\omega_i)\) of the next point \(p'\), and the equation becomes recursive: \[ L_{out}(p, \omega_o) = L_e(p, \omega_o) + \int_{H^2} f(\omega_i \to \omega_o)\, L_{out}(p', -\omega_i) \cos\theta_i \, d\omega_i \] If \(L_k\) is the light that bounced exactly \(k\) times before reaching the camera, the image is \(L_0 + L_1 + L_2 + \dots\) Part 3 computed \(L_0\) and \(L_1\). Part 4 adds the indirect light \(L_2 + L_3 + \dots\)

If we took \(N\) directions at each bounce, we would need \(N^k\) rays for \(k\) bounces. Path tracing follows only one random direction at each bounce, and we reduce the noise with many samples per pixel. This direction comes from DiffuseBSDF::sample_f. It uses the cosine-weighted hemisphere sampler, writes the direction \(\omega_i\) and its pdf \(\cos\theta / \pi\), and returns \(f = \rho / \pi\). With this pdf, \(f \cos\theta / pdf = \rho\): each bounce multiplies the light by the color of the surface.

at_least_one_bounce_radiance(r, isect) works like this:

  1. we compute the direct light \(L_1\) at the hit point with one_bounce_radiance;
  2. each ray has a depth, the number of bounces it can still do. In raytrace_pixel, camera rays start with depth = max_ray_depth. If depth \(\le 1\), we return \(L_1\);
  3. Russian roulette decides if the path continues;
  4. sample_f gives a new direction (in local coordinates), its pdf and \(f\);
  5. we cast a ray from the hit point in this direction (converted with o2w), with min_t = EPS_F and depth - 1;
  6. if this ray hits a point \(p'\), we call at_least_one_bounce_radiance on \(p'\) and add \(f \cdot L(p') \cdot \cos\theta / (pdf \cdot p_{cont})\), where \(p_{cont}\) is the continuation probability of the Russian roulette.

We never add zero_bounce_radiance at \(p'\). If the new ray hits the light, this light is already counted in \(L_1\) by light sampling, and adding its emission would count it twice. est_radiance_global_illumination returns only \(L_0\) when max_ray_depth = 0, and \(L_0\) plus at_least_one_bounce_radiance otherwise.

Accumulated and unaccumulated bounces (isAccumBounces, the -o flag). When it is true, every level of the recursion adds its own \(L_1\), so we get \(L_0 + L_1 + \dots + L_m\). When it is false, only the last level (depth = 1) returns its \(L_1\), the other levels only pass on the recursive part, and \(L_0\) is not added. The result is then \(L_m\), the \(m\)-th bounce alone.

Russian roulette. At each bounce, the path continues with probability \(p_{cont} = 0.7\) (coin_flip(0.7)), so it stops with probability 0.3, as suggested in the instructions. When it continues, we divide its contribution by 0.7. The expected value does not change: \(0.7 \cdot \frac{X}{0.7} + 0.3 \cdot 0 = X\), so the estimator is still unbiased, and paths do about \(1/0.3 \approx 3.3\) indirect bounces on average. For the first bounce (depth = max_ray_depth) we use \(p_{cont} = 1\), so when max_ray_depth \(> 1\) we always trace at least one indirect bounce.

Part 5: Adaptive Sampling

What adaptive sampling is

In Part 4, every pixel used the same number of samples. But some pixels converge much faster than others. A pixel on a wall that sees the light directly gets almost the same value from every path, so a few samples are enough. A pixel on the ceiling or in a shadow only gets light from random indirect paths: most paths bring little light and a few bring a lot, so it needs many more samples to lose its noise. Adaptive sampling uses statistics to decide, for each pixel, when it has enough samples. It stops sampling the easy pixels early and keeps the full sample budget for the hard ones.

After \(n\) samples of a pixel, we look at their illuminances \(x_1, \dots, x_n\), with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\). The true value of the pixel is in the interval \(\mu \pm I\) with 95% confidence, where \[ I = 1.96 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \] \(I\) is small when the samples vary little or when there are many of them. We consider that the pixel has converged when \[ I \le maxTolerance \cdot \mu \] with maxTolerance = 0.05. So we stop when we are 95% sure that the mean is within 5% of the true value. The tolerance is relative to \(\mu\): an error of the same size is much easier to see in a dark pixel than in a bright one.

Walk through our implementation

Everything happens in PathTracer::raytrace_pixel(x, y). The -s value (ns_aa) is now the maximum number of samples per pixel.

  1. We keep a running sum of the radiances (col), the number of samples \(n\), and two sums of illuminances: \(s_1 = \sum x_k\) and \(s_2 = \sum x_k^2\). With these two sums we never store the individual samples.
  2. In a loop that runs while \(n\) is below ns_aa, we pick a random point in the pixel with gridSampler, normalize it by the image size, and generate a camera ray with depth = max_ray_depth. Then we call est_radiance_global_illumination, add the radiance to col, and add its illuminance \(x_k\) (radiance.illum()) to \(s_1\) and \(x_k^2\) to \(s_2\).
  3. Computing the statistics after every sample would cost too much, so we only test convergence after each batch of samplesPerBatch samples (64 in our renders, set with -a). The mean and the variance are \[ \mu = \frac{s_1}{n}, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\left(s_2 - \frac{s_1^2}{n}\right) \] Because of floating-point rounding, \(\sigma^2\) can come out slightly negative when all the samples are almost equal, so we clamp it to 0.
  4. We compute \(I = 1.96 \sqrt{\sigma^2 / n}\). If \(I \le maxTolerance \cdot \mu\), we stop sampling this pixel. Otherwise we continue with the next batch, up to ns_aa samples.
  5. At the end, we write the real number of samples \(n\) to sampleCountBuffer (used for the sampling rate image) and the average radiance col / n to the pixel.

Both rendered images are free of noise, even with only 1 sample per light. The sampling rate images show where the work went:

  • Red: the ceiling, the shadows on the floor under the bunny and the spheres, and the parts of the objects that face away from the light (the lower half of the bunny, the dark sides of the spheres). These points do not see the light directly, or only see part of it. The top corners of the ceiling, far from the light, are the hardest.
  • Green: the transition zones, like the edges of the shadows, the upper part of the back wall near the ceiling, and the parts of the objects that are lit at a grazing angle.

The final renders are essentially noise-free, with smooth shadows and color bleeding from the red and blue walls, while most of the image used far fewer than 2048 samples.
